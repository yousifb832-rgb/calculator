The idea in 30 seconds

Your hand already works this way

Inside your arm, muscles pull long cords called tendons, and the tendons bend your fingers. Our glove copies that.

1

The motor winds the string

Like a fishing reel. A small motor (a servo) turns a round wheel and winds up a strong fishing line.

2

The string bends the finger

The line runs along the palm side of the finger to the fingertip. Shorter string = bent finger.

3

The elastic opens it again

An elastic band on the back of the finger pulls it straight when the motor lets the string out.

A tiny computer called an Arduino tells the motor what to do: close slowly, hold, open slowly, rest, and repeat, as many times as you choose. Your laptop can start it, stop it and save the results.

Why one finger first?

A wooden finger is cheap, safe and easy to fix. If something is wrong you see it immediately. And the numbers you measure on it tell you which motor to buy for the full glove, so you don't waste money.

Part A · Build the wooden finger

Builder

  1. Cut four blocks

    From the 20 × 20 mm wooden stick, cut 80, 45, 28 and 22 mm. Sand the ends flat and round the tip of the smallest one.

    Why: these are the palm and the three bones of a finger, a little bigger than real ones.

    Watch out: cut straight. A crooked cut makes the finger bend sideways.

  2. Mark the palm side

    Draw a straight pencil line along one face of every block and write V on it. V is the palm side.

    Why: the hinges and the string go on the V side, the elastic on the other side. Marking now avoids mistakes later.

  3. Join the blocks with three small hinges

    Lay two blocks end to end, V side up, touching. Put a hinge across the joint so its round pin sits exactly over the gap. Drill small 1.5 mm holes first, then screw. Do this three times.

    Why: these are your knuckles. When the finger is straight, the blocks touch on the back, so it can't bend the wrong way, just like a real finger.

    Watch out: no pre-drilled holes = split wood. Check that each joint bends easily and doesn't wobble sideways.

  4. Screw in the little string rings

    On the V line, screw in small screw eyes: two on the base, one in the middle of each of the next two blocks, and one near the fingertip. Turn them so all the holes line up in a straight row.

    Why: the string slides through these rings. They keep it close to the finger, like the bands that hold your real tendons.

    Watch out: if the rings aren't in a straight line, the string rubs and the finger twists.

  5. Add the elastic on the back

    Put three more screw eyes on the back side and one screw on the base. Tie the elastic cord at the fingertip, run it through the eyes and wrap it twice around the screw.

    Test: bend the finger with one fingertip and let go. It should spring back straight in under a second, but still be easy to bend.

    Watch out: too tight and the motor will struggle; too loose and the finger won't open.

  6. Fix the finger to the board

    Lay the finger on its side on a wooden board and screw the base block down from below. Tape a paper protractor (angle printout) under the first joint.

    Why on its side? It bends across the board, so you can film it from above and read the angles.

  7. Make the drum (the reel)

    Glue a bottle cap (25 to 30 mm across) onto the round plastic arm that comes with the servo. Add a flat disc on each side so the string can't fall off. Drill a tiny hole near the edge to tie the string.

    Why a drum and not the arm? A round drum pulls evenly the whole way; an arm doesn't.

    Measure the cap's width and write it down. You need it later.

  8. Fix the servo to the board

    Screw the servo onto a small block of wood, 10 to 15 cm behind the finger, so the drum lines up with the string. Don't push the drum on yet; that happens in Part D.

  9. Tie the string

    Cut 60 cm of fishing line. Tie it to the fingertip ring with a strong knot plus a drop of superglue. Thread it back through every ring on the V side. Slide one bead onto the string between the two base rings; don't fix it yet.

    Pull the free end gently with your hand: the finger should curl. Let go: it should straighten. If yes, the mechanics work.

Part B · Connect the wires

Coder

Only 8 connections. Do them with everything unplugged. The yellow numbers in the picture match the list.

Never power the motor from the Arduino

The Arduino has a pin called 5V. Don't connect the servo to it. The motor's current would make the Arduino restart and could damage your laptop's USB port. The motor always gets its power from the adapter.

Before you switch on: 3 quick checks with the multimeter
  1. Beep test between + and − rails: it must not beep (no short circuit).
  2. Beep test between Arduino GND and the − rail: it must beep.
  3. Plug in the adapter only (servo unplugged), switch on: the rails should read about 5 V. Switch off: 0 V.

Part C · Put the program on the Arduino

Coder

You don't need to write any code. It's ready. You only copy it to the Arduino, which is called uploading.

  1. Install the Arduino IDE

    Free from arduino.cc → Software. "IDE" just means the program where you open and upload Arduino code.

  2. Get the code

    Download the code files from the Code section below and unzip them. Keep each .ino file inside its own folder with the same name.

  3. Plug in the Arduino

    Use a USB cable that carries data (some cables only charge). If Windows doesn't find it, search for and install the CH340 driver; most cheap Nano boards need it.

  4. Tell the IDE which board you have

    Tools → Board → Arduino Nano.
    Tools → Processor → ATmega328P (Old Bootloader).
    Tools → Port → the COM port that appeared when you plugged in.

  5. Open and upload the test program

    Open servo_calibration.ino. Click the round → Upload button. Wait for "Done uploading".

    Error "not in sync"? Change Processor to the other ATmega328P choice and try again.

  6. Open the Serial Monitor

    Tools → Serial Monitor. At the bottom, choose 115200 baud and New Line. You should see Servo calibration ready. This window is how you type commands to the Arduino.

Part D · Test it together

Both

  1. Measure the pull with a luggage scale

    Motor off. Hook the luggage scale to the free end of the string. First make an ink dot on the string right where it leaves the last ring. Now pull slowly until the finger is fully bent. Read the scale (in kg) and measure how far the ink dot moved (in mm).

    Do it 3 times and use the average. Type your numbers into the motor finder just below this list.

  2. Find the two safe angles: OPEN and CLOSED

    Switch motor power on: the servo turns to 90°. Now push the drum onto the servo and tie the string to it, slightly loose. In the Serial Monitor type + or - (then Enter) to turn 2° at a time.

    OPEN = the angle where the string just becomes tight and the finger is still straight.
    CLOSED = keep going 5° at a time until the finger is bent enough, or until the motor starts to hum. Stop there.

    Write both numbers in your notebook. They are the most important numbers of the project.

  3. Fix the safety bead

    With the motor at CLOSED, slide the bead to 3 mm in front of the back ring and glue it there. Now the string can never be pulled much further than CLOSED, even if the program made a mistake.

  4. Upload the real program with your numbers

    Open robotic_glove.ino. Near the top, change these lines to your two angles, and switch off the power sensor you haven't built yet:

    const float FINGERS_OPEN_DEG   = 20;   // ← your OPEN
const float FINGERS_CLOSED_DEG = 150;  // ← your CLOSED
const bool  USE_MOTOR_SENSE  = false;  // ← false for Stage 1

    Upload it, like before.

  5. First real run

    Open the Serial Monitor. One person keeps a hand on the switch. Type each line and press Enter:

    MODE FINGERS
SPEED 20
RANGE 50
REPS 3
START

    The finger closes halfway, slowly, three times. It works? Try RANGE 100 and then SPEED 40. Type STOP anytime.

  6. The 100-times test

    Type REPS 100, SPEED 40, RANGE 100, START. It takes about 14 minutes. Every 25 times, stop and check: is the motor only warm (not hot)? Are the knots holding? Has the ink dot moved?

    If it does 100 out of 100, Stage 1 is a success.

How it works it out

Force for one finger = kg × 9.81. For four fingers it multiplies by 4, then adds 50 % for the glove fabric and friction in the tubes. The drum is sized so the motor turns 130° to pull your string distance. A motor should work at no more than 40 % of its maximum strength, so it stays cool and lasts. The finder picks the smallest motor that stays under 40 %. The thumb moves less, so a small metal-gear servo is accepted up to 60 %.

Stage 1 is finished when…
  • The finger bends clearly at all three joints.
  • It goes back straight after every bend.
  • It did 100 bends in a row without a problem.
  • The motor was only warm, never too hot to touch.
  • STOP and the switch both stop it straight away.
  • You wrote your OPEN, CLOSED and motor-finder answer in the notebook.

Safety

Six golden rules

Read these before you switch anything on. Say them out loud once. They're short so you remember them.

  1. Wood only, never patientsTest on the wooden finger and the wooden hand. Not on a patient, not on a friend's hand.
  2. Two people, one red switchOne person runs the test. The other keeps a hand near the switch the whole time.
  3. Motor power comes from the adapterNever from the Arduino's 5V pin, and only from a 5 V adapter.
  4. Start slow and smallAfter any change, start with SPEED 20 and RANGE 50. Go faster only when that works.
  5. The switch stops it, it doesn't open itWhen power is cut the motor freezes where it is. To free a finger, unclip or untie the string.
  6. Hot, smelly or buzzing? Switch off firstPress the switch first, think second. Then find the cause before you try again.
The code

You don't write it. You upload it.

There are two Arduino programs and one laptop app. All three are finished and tested in a simulator.

What the program does, like a recipe

  1. Waitfor someone to press START
  2. Closethe finger slowly
  3. Holdit closed for a second
  4. Openthe finger slowly
  5. Restcount one, and repeat until done
And all the time, hundreds of times per second, it checks:
  • Was STOP pressed? Then open gently and stop.
  • Did the red switch cut the power? Then freeze and wait. Only START wakes it again.
  • Never go past your OPEN and CLOSED angles, whatever anyone types.

Stuck?

Quick fixes for the usual problems

Everyone hits these the first time. Find your problem, try the fix, and change only one thing at a time.

The motor shakes or twitches

Wire 7 (Arduino GND to the − rail) is missing or loose. Check it with the beep test.

The Arduino keeps restarting

The motor is getting power from the Arduino. Move it to the adapter's rails.

Upload fails: "not in sync"

Change Tools → Processor to the other ATmega328P choice. Close the Serial Monitor. Try another cable.

No COM port appears

Install the CH340 driver. Your cable may be charge-only; try another.

The motor hums and gets hot when closed

CLOSED is too far. Make it 5 to 10° smaller. Or the elastic is too tight.

The finger doesn't open fully

The string is too tight at OPEN, or the elastic is too weak.

The motor turns but the finger hardly moves

The string is slipping on the drum or a knot is loose. Tie it through the drum hole and glue the knots.

The finger bends sideways

The string rings aren't in a straight line. Turn them back in line.

Still stuck?

Write down three things: what you expected, what actually happened, and what you already tried. Bring that to your instructor, or paste it to me. With those three, almost any problem can be solved.
