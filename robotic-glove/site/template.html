    Robotic Glove Build Guide

    Design and Development of a Low-Cost Automatic Robotic Glove for Finger Movement Assistance and Rehabilitation

    Two small servos pull thin tendons along the palm side of the fingers to bend them. Elastic bands on the back of the hand straighten them again. An Arduino repeats the movement inside safe limits, and a Python app on your PC starts it, shows what it is doing and records every test in a CSV file. This page is written as your engineering supervisor: it reviews your concept, fixes the weak points, and takes you from zero to a working prototype, one stage at a time.

    A

    Mechanical design

    How the glove bends the fingers, where every part goes, and how to build it.

    A1. How a tendon bends a finger

    Each finger has three joints: MCP (knuckle at the base), PIP (middle) and DIP (near the tip). A tendon tied at the fingertip runs along the palm side through small guide tubes. Because the tendon sits a small distance d below each joint's axis, pulling it creates a turning effect (torque = force × d) at all three joints, and the finger curls. This is called flexion.

    One tendon drives three joints, so the finger is underactuated: the joints share the movement according to how stiff each one is. That is good for grasping, because a finger that touches an object stops while the others keep closing. When the servo unwinds, elastic on the back of the finger pulls it straight again. That is extension.

    A2. Where the servo motors go

    Put both servos in an actuator pack: a small board or plastic box that sits on the table during all artificial-hand testing. Do not mount motors on the hand. A motor on the hand adds weight, heat and pinch points, and its cable pulls on the glove. The pack connects to the glove only through Bowden sheaths, so it can later be strapped to the forearm if your instructor approves a wearable demonstration.

    A3. Tendon routing on the glove

    • Fingertip anchor: a small cap of strong fabric or thin leather sewn over each glove fingertip. The tendon is tied to it. A cap spreads the pull over the whole tip, so the glove fabric does not tear.
    • Guides: two per finger (proximal and middle phalanx), one on the thumb. Short tubes, 8 to 10 mm long, on the centre line of the finger. Never over a joint crease, or the finger cannot bend.
    • Palm plate: where the sheaths end. The tendon leaves the sheath here and runs free to the guides.
    • Thumb: anchor at the thumb tip, one guide on the proximal phalanx, sheath end at the base of the thumb. Moving the sheath end toward the base of the little finger adds more opposition (thumb crossing the palm). Try two positions on the artificial hand.

    A4. Keeping the tendons from tangling

    • One tendon per guide and per sheath. Tendons never cross each other.
    • Drums have flanges on both sides, so a slack line cannot jump off.
    • Set the OPEN angle so each tendon is just tight with the fingers straight. A slightly tight tendon cannot form loops.
    • Colour-tag each tendon end with a marker: F1 to F4 and T.
    • Keep braided line away from Velcro hooks; it catches on them. Cover hook Velcro near tendons.
    • Bundle the sheaths with tape every 10 cm and avoid bends tighter than about 5 cm radius. Tight bends add friction.
    • Keep the drum area covered so loose ends cannot wrap around it.

    A5. How far and how hard: the two numbers that size everything

    Travel (how far the tendon must move)

    x = d × (θMCP + θPIP + θDIP)  [angles in radians]

    Example: d = 10 mm, a functional grasp of MCP 60°, PIP 70°, DIP 40° = 170° = 2.97 rad, so x ≈ 30 mm.

    drum radius r = x ÷ θservo

    If the servo turns 130° (2.27 rad) between OPEN and CLOSED: r = 30 ÷ 2.27 ≈ 13 mm, a drum about 26 mm across.

    Force (how hard the tendon can pull)

    F = T ÷ r    T[N·m] = kg·cm × 0.0981

    A smaller drum gives more force but less travel. A bigger drum gives more travel but less force. Force × travel is fixed by the motor, so choose the drum from the travel you need, then check that the force is enough.

    Rule of thumb: in continuous use, load a hobby servo to no more than about 40 % of its stall torque.

    A6. Comfort and adjustability

    • Choose a glove that fits snugly: tendons in a loose glove move sideways and lose force.
    • Velcro wrist strap and a Velcro strap around the palm hold the glove in place against the tendon pull.
    • A 3 to 5 mm foam pad under the palm plate. Smooth, rounded tube ends (sand them).
    • Tendon length: adjusted at the tendon bar, one finger at a time, without taking the glove off.
    • Elastic tension: adjustable with a sliding buckle or a knot loop at the back-of-hand strap.
    • Nothing heavy on the hand. Motors, electronics and cables stay in the pack.

    A7. Building the glove, step by step

    1. Mark the glove

      Put the glove on the artificial hand (or a team member's hand, only for marking). With a fabric marker, draw the centre line of each finger and the three joint creases on the palm side.

    2. Sew the fingertip caps

      Cut five pieces of strong fabric or thin leather, about 25 × 35 mm. Fold each over a fingertip, palm side, and sew it with strong nylon thread using a box-and-X stitch. Leave a small loop of thread or a tiny button on the palm side to tie the tendon to.

    3. Make and fit the guides

      Cut PTFE tube (2 mm inside) or empty ballpoint refill tubes into 8 to 10 mm pieces and sand the ends smooth. Sew each one on the centre line over the middle of the proximal and middle phalanx, with 4 to 6 stitches around the tube, then add a drop of fabric glue. Check that every guide is between two crease lines.

    4. Make the palm plate

      Cut a 50 × 60 mm plate from 2 mm plastic sheet (or a stiff food-container lid). Drill five 4 mm holes where the tendons leave the fingers and the thumb base. Glue an M3 washer over each hole on the finger side: the sheath pushes against the washer, the tendon passes through its hole. Glue foam under the plate, then sew hook Velcro on the glove palm and loop Velcro on the plate.

    5. Fit the elastic return

      Sew one end of 8 to 10 mm flat elastic to the back of each fingertip cap. Run it along the back of the finger to a strap across the back of the hand and fix it with a sliding buckle or safety pin. Tension rule: from half-closed, the finger straightens by itself in about one second.

    6. Tie in the tendons

      Cut five tendons of about 80 cm. Tie each to its fingertip anchor with a Palomar knot and add a drop of superglue. Thread it through its guides, through the palm plate washer, and into its sheath.

    7. Route the sheaths

      Cut five PTFE sheaths to reach the actuator pack (30 to 50 cm). Tape them together every 10 cm. The wrist strap holds the bundle so its weight never pulls on the palm plate.

    A8. Building the actuator pack, step by step

    1. Base

      A 20 × 12 cm piece of plywood, acrylic or PVC board, or a plastic food box with a lid.

    2. Drums

      Glue a 25 to 30 mm bottle cap or spool section on the round servo horn with epoxy and add two small screws through the horn. Add a flange on each side. Drill a 1.5 mm hole near the rim to anchor the line.

    3. Mount the servos

      Screw each servo by its tabs onto wooden risers so the drum edge is level with the tendon path.

    4. Finger line

      Drum → main line → fishing snap swivel (quick-release) → main line through the travel-stop block → tendon bar. The bar is a 40 × 15 mm strip with four M3 screws and washers; each finger tendon is clamped under one washer, so its length can be adjusted.

    5. Sheath stop

      A block with four holes and washers, like the palm plate. The four sheaths push against it.

    6. Thumb line

      Drum → snap swivel → stop block → bead or crimp sleeve on the line → sheath stop → sheath.

    7. Set the travel stops

      After calibration, set the gap between the tendon bar (or bead) and its stop block so it touches the block about 3 mm of tendon travel after the CLOSED angle. The stop then only acts if something goes wrong.

    8. Cover and cables

      Cover the drums. Lead the servo cables out through one hole and fix them with a cable tie as strain relief.

    Cut list and layout

    Build the finger (Student 1 leads)

    1. Cut the four blocks

      From the 20 × 20 mm stick cut the base 80 mm, proximal 45 mm, middle 28 mm and distal 22 mm. Use a try square and a fine saw, then sand the ends flat and square. Crooked ends make the finger bend sideways. Round the distal tip and soften the long edges; later this finger goes inside a glove.

    2. Mark the palm face

      Draw a centre line along one face of every block and write V (volar, palm side). Hinges and tendon eyes go on the V face. The elastic goes on the opposite face, the back.

    3. Fit the three hinges

      Lay the base and proximal blocks end to end, V face up, ends touching. Centre a mini hinge across the joint so its pin lies exactly over the gap. Drill 1.5 mm pilot holes first (without them the wood splits), then screw. Repeat for proximal–middle (PIP) and middle–distal (DIP).

      Check: each joint bends at least 90° toward the V side, has no sideways wobble, and stops when straight because the blocks touch on the back. That stop protects against over-straightening, like the ligaments of a real finger.

    4. Fit the tendon eyes

      Screw small eyes into the V centre line: a rear eye 15 mm from the back end of the base, a front eye 20 mm before the MCP hinge, one at the middle of the proximal block, one at the middle of the middle block, and the anchor eye 10 mm from the fingertip. Turn every eye so its hole lines up along the centre line. The height of an eye's hole above the hinge pin is the moment arm d: aim for 6 to 8 mm, the same at every joint.

    5. Fit the elastic return

      On the back face, add eyes at the middle of the proximal and middle blocks and at the fingertip, and a screw on the base block about 190 mm behind the tip. Tie the 2 mm elastic cord to the tip eye, thread it through the eyes and wrap it twice round the screw. Tension rule: the finger straightens by itself from any position in under a second, but one fingertip can still bend it easily.

    6. Mount the finger on the board

      Lay it on its side and screw the base block down with two screws from below. Tape a printed paper protractor under the MCP joint, centred on the hinge pin.

    7. Make the drum

      Glue a 25 to 30 mm bottle cap or spool piece centred on a servo horn (epoxy, plus two small screws through the horn). Add a flange on each side so the line cannot slip off. Drill a 1.5 mm hole near the rim. Measure the diameter where the line wraps and write down r = diameter ÷ 2.

    8. Mount the servo

      Screw the SG90 by its tabs onto a small wooden block, 10 to 15 cm behind the rear eye, with the drum in line with the tendon. Do not press the drum onto the servo shaft yet; you will do that during calibration.

    9. Tie the tendon

      Cut 60 cm of braided line. Tie it to the fingertip eye with a Palomar knot and add a drop of superglue. Thread it through all the V eyes from tip to rear. Slide one bead onto the line between the rear eye and the front eye; do not fix it yet. Leave the free end loose for the force test.

    Measure, calibrate and run (both students)

    1. Measure tendon force and travel by hand

      Motor not connected. With the finger straight, make an ink mark on the line exactly at the rear eye. Hook the luggage scale to the free end and pull slowly in line with the tendon. Stop at four positions: about a quarter, half, three-quarters and full flexion (full = the most the finger can bend before blocks touch). At each stop, read the scale, read the joint angles on the protractor, and measure how far the ink mark has moved from the rear eye: that distance is the tendon travel x. Do 3 trials and average. Newtons = kg reading × 9.81.

    2. Upload the calibration sketch

      Upload servo_calibration.ino (chapter C), open the Serial Monitor at 115200 baud with "Newline". Switch on servo power: the servo goes to 90°. Now press the drum onto the shaft, tie the tendon through the drum hole with 2 to 3 wraps, leaving it slightly slack.

    3. Find OPEN and CLOSED

      Type + or - to nudge 2° at a time (or type an angle) toward the side that winds the line. OPEN is the angle where the tendon just becomes tight while the finger is still straight. Keep going 5° at a time: CLOSED is your target flexion, or the angle just before the servo starts to strain or buzz, whichever comes first. Write both down. Sanity check: |CLOSED − OPEN| × π/180 × r should be close to the travel you measured by hand.

    4. Set the travel stop

      With the servo at CLOSED, slide the bead to 3 mm in front of the rear eye (finger side) and fix it with a crimp, a knot or a drop of glue. Now the line can never be pulled more than 3 mm beyond CLOSED, whatever the software does.

    5. First powered run

      In robotic_glove.ino set FINGERS_OPEN_DEG and FINGERS_CLOSED_DEG to your numbers, and set USE_MOTOR_SENSE = false until the A0 divider is built. Upload, open the Serial Monitor and type, one line at a time: MODE FINGERS, SPEED 20, RANGE 50, REPS 3, START. Watch closely, hand on the switch. Then try RANGE 80 and RANGE 100, then SPEED 40. The thumb servo is not connected yet; FINGERS mode never moves it.

    6. Endurance: 100 cycles

      Settings SPEED 40, RANGE 100, HOLD 1000, REST 1000. One cycle takes about 8.5 s, so 100 cycles take about 14 minutes. Every 25 cycles: STOP, touch the servo case, check the knots, the eyes and the ink mark, and write it down. If the PC app is ready, run it from the app so the CSV records the session.

    7. Decide the motor

      Enter your measured force and travel into the calculator below. Write the result and the reason in your logbook. That decision is the main output of Stage 1.

    Common mistakes in Stage 1

    • Powering the servo from the Arduino 5V pin: the Arduino resets and the servo twitches.
    • No common ground: the servo does nothing or shakes.
    • Tying the tendon to the servo arm instead of a drum.
    • Eyes not in one straight line: the tendon rubs and the finger twists sideways.
    • Hinge pin not exactly on the joint line: the joint binds.
    • Elastic too strong: the servo cannot reach CLOSED, buzzes and gets hot.
    • Calibrating with the tendon already tied tight: the servo jumps to 90° at power-up and yanks the finger.
    • No pilot holes: the small blocks split.
    • Writing measurements down "later". Write them immediately, with the date.

    B

    Electronics design

    Two power domains that share one ground, an emergency switch that cuts only motor power, and a few cheap parts that prevent most failures.

    Logic power comes from USB (your PC, or a phone power bank when you run without a PC). Motor power comes from a separate regulated 5 V adapter. The two meet only at ground. This keeps servo current spikes away from the Arduino and the PC's USB port, and lets the Arduino keep running and reporting while the emergency switch has cut the motors.

    Choosing the power supply

    SG90: about 0.1 to 0.3 A while moving, up to about 0.7 A when stalled. MG996R: about 0.5 to 0.9 A moving, up to about 2.5 A stalled. With both servos the worst case is about 3.2 A, so use a regulated 5 V adapter of at least 3 A, and 5 A if you use an MG996R. Check the label says 5 V DC and measure it: some cheap adapters give 5.5 V or more with no load. Servos are rated 4.8 to 6 V; never use 9 V or 12 V.

    The emergency stop

    • Hardware: a latching switch in the motor + line, after the fuse. A red 22 mm mushroom E-stop from an electrical-panel shop is best; a 5 A rocker switch is acceptable. It must stay off when pressed and need a deliberate action to reset.
    • Placement: on top of the control box, within reach of the operator. During any test one person keeps a hand near it.
    • Software: A0 sees the motor rail fall below 3.5 V. The program stops all motion, stops sending servo pulses, reports EVENT ESTOP, and blinks the LED fast. When power returns it still does nothing until someone presses START once to re-arm, and START again to run. It never restarts by itself.
    • What it cannot do: open the hand. See the quick-release in chapter G.

    Why a common ground matters

    The servo reads its signal as a voltage measured against its own ground. If the Arduino's ground and the adapter's ground are not joined, the servo sees a meaningless voltage: it twitches, drifts, or ignores you. Joining the grounds gives both sides the same 0 V reference. Only grounds are joined; the two + supplies stay separate.

    ProblemProtection

    Assembly order and first power-on

    1. Wire everything with all power off

      Adapter unplugged, USB unplugged.

    2. Check with a multimeter

      Continuity: Arduino GND to GND rail, adapter − to GND rail. Resistance between motor + rail and GND rail should not be close to 0 Ω.

    3. Power the adapter alone

      Servos unplugged. E-stop released: 4.8 to 5.3 V on the rail. E-stop pressed: 0 V.

    4. Connect USB and upload

      Upload the calibration sketch first. Then plug in the servos (motor power off), then switch motor power on.

    5. Watch, smell, touch

      For the first minute, watch for heat, smell or smoke. If anything is wrong, press the E-stop.

    How the program works, in plain language

    1. No delay() anywhere

    loop() runs thousands of times per second. Each time it reads the buttons, checks motor power, reads PC commands and moves the servos a tiny step, using millis() (time since power-up) to know how far along each movement is. Because it never waits, STOP and the E-stop are noticed within milliseconds, even in the middle of a movement.

    2. Calibration and the safety clamp

    Section 2 holds your OPEN and CLOSED angles. Every position the program sends goes through writeAxis(), which calls clampToAxis(): any value outside OPEN…CLOSED is cut to the nearest limit. A wrong PC command, a typing mistake or a bug cannot drive past your calibrated angles.

    3. Speed and smooth motion

    Speed is the average speed in degrees per second (10 to 90). A movement of Δ degrees takes Δ ÷ speed seconds. Inside that time the position follows an S-curve, (1 − cos(π·u)) ÷ 2: it starts slowly, speeds up, and slows down again before stopping. No jerks for the tendon, the finger or the gears. Positions are sent in microseconds, about 0.1° steps.

    4. Angle (range of motion)

    RANGE is a percentage of your calibrated range. The flex target is OPEN + (CLOSED − OPEN) × RANGE ÷ 100. RANGE 50 gives half the flexion; RANGE 100 gives exactly CLOSED, never more. Start every new setup at 50.

    5. Repetitions, hold and rest

    One repetition is FLEX → HOLD → EXTEND. The counter goes up when the extension finishes, and the program sends EVENT REP n. After the last one it sends EVENT DONE n and returns to IDLE. HOLD is the time held closed, REST the time open between repetitions.

    6. Modes and the thumb

    FULL moves both servos, FINGERS only Servo F, THUMB only Servo T. In FULL mode the thumb starts 0.3 s after the fingers when closing, and the fingers wait 0.3 s for the thumb when opening, so the thumb is never trapped under the fingers.

    7. Buttons

    Buttons connect a pin to GND. INPUT_PULLUP holds the pin HIGH until a press pulls it LOW. A press only counts after it has been stable for 30 ms (debouncing), because metal contacts bounce. STOP is checked before START in every loop.

    8. Emergency stop behaviour

    Every 5 ms the program reads A0. If the motor rail stays below 3.5 V for 50 ms, it enters ESTOP: movement plans are cancelled and the servos are detached (no more pulses). Re-arming needs the voltage above 4.2 V and a START press. The servos are re-attached at the OPEN angle.

    Using it

    1. Fill in Test ID, tester and notes (for example "artificial hand, glove v1, MG996R").
    2. Choose mode, speed, range, repetitions, hold and rest.
    3. Press START. The app sends all settings and then START.
    4. Watch the state, the repetition counter, both servo angles and the motor supply voltage.
    5. When the session ends (completed, stopped, emergency stop or cable unplugged) the app saves one row to glove_results.csv and moves the Test ID on (T03 → T04).
    6. After an emergency stop: release the switch, then press Re-arm.

    How the app works

    A background thread reads lines from the USB port and puts them in a queue. Every 50 ms the window takes the waiting lines from the queue and updates the labels. STATUS lines update the display; EVENT lines start and end the test record. Sessions are recorded from the Arduino's own events, so a session started with the physical START button is logged too.

    Safety layers, from the design outwards

    1. Limited force by designThe motor and drum you chose set the largest pull that can ever exist (stall torque ÷ drum radius). Do not use a motor much stronger than you need.
    2. Mechanical travel stopsBead and tendon bar hit stop blocks 3 mm beyond CLOSED. They work even if the code is wrong.
    3. Software limitsEvery position is clamped to the calibrated OPEN…CLOSED range; speed, range and timing are bounded.
    4. Controlled stopSTOP button and STOP command return smoothly to OPEN.
    5. Emergency power cutLatching switch in the motor supply; the program needs a deliberate re-arm.
    6. Quick releaseVelcro palm plate and snap clips remove all tendon force in seconds, with or without power.
    7. ProcedureTwo-person rule, checklist before every session, no unattended running, artificial hand only.
    Important

    Cutting power does not open the hand. A hobby servo has a high gear ratio, so the elastic usually cannot turn it backwards; without power, the fingers stay where they were. That is why the quick-release is a required part, not an extra.

    Protection against excessive force and finger entrapment

    • Fingers close before the thumb and open after it (firmware), so the thumb cannot be trapped.
    • Guides sit between the creases; nothing rigid crosses a joint, so no skin can be pinched in a closing joint.
    • Tube ends are sanded round; no sharp edges face the skin.
    • Drums are covered; the actuator pack is away from the hand.
    • Nothing is worn under the glove: no rings, watches or bracelets.
    • Low speed for new settings: SPEED 20 and RANGE 50 first, then step up.

    SG90 limitations, and whether you need stronger motors

    SG90 (and MG90S)

    • Stall torque about 1.8 kg·cm at 4.8 V. Continuously, use about 40 % of that.
    • Plastic gears strip under shock or stall (MG90S has metal gears, same torque).
    • No feedback to the Arduino: it never knows whether the position was reached.
    • Resolution about 1°, with a small dead band; cheap units vary a lot.
    • Draws current and heats while holding against a load (HOLD phase).
    • Only 180° travel. Continuous-rotation versions (FS90R) are not suitable.

    Verdict

    Yes, the four-finger tendon needs a stronger motor. One SG90 gives about 1.4 N per finger on a 13 mm drum, which is unlikely to be enough once a glove, guides, sheaths and elastics are added. An MG996R gives about 7 N per finger with the same drum.

    MG996R notes: heavier (55 g), noisier, stall current up to about 2.5 A (hence the 5 A adapter), and clones vary, so test yours in Stage 2. The SG90 stays a good choice for the Stage 1 finger and the thumb of the artificial hand.

    Troubleshooting

    When something goes wrong, work through it systematically instead of changing things at random.

    1. Make it happen again

      Write down exactly what you did and what happened. A problem you can repeat is half solved.

    2. Measure, do not guess

      Voltage on the servo rail, the Serial Monitor output, a slow-motion video of the mechanism.

    3. Split the system in two

      Unhook the tendon and run the servo alone: still wrong means electrical or code. Pull the tendon by hand: still stiff means mechanical. Run the PC app on the simulator: still wrong means the app.

    4. Change one thing at a time

      Then test again. Two changes at once hide which one helped.

    5. Write down the cause and the fix

      In the logbook. It goes into your report.

    Words to use, and to avoid

    Say: "assistive movement prototype for rehabilitation research", "tested on an artificial hand", "educational prototype".

    Do not say: "treats stroke or ALS", "clinically effective", "a medical device", "safe for patients".

    Questions examiners are likely to ask

    QuestionShort answer
    Why tendons instead of motors on each joint?Light and soft on the hand; motors stay off the body; one motor can move several joints.
    How do you limit the force on the finger?Motor and drum choice bound the maximum pull; travel stops; clamped angles; and we measured the force needed.
    What happens if power fails during flexion?Motion stops; the servos hold, so the quick-release opens the hand; the program will not restart by itself.
    Why not test on patients?An unvalidated prototype; patient use needs risk analysis, ethics approval and clinical evaluation.
    What would a real medical device need?A quality system (ISO 13485), risk management (ISO 14971), electrical safety (IEC 60601-1), software lifecycle (IEC 62304), skin-contact biocompatibility (ISO 10993) and clinical evaluation.
    How do you know it is repeatable?CSV logs of N sessions, angle spread from video, endurance results.

    Glossary

    Technical words used in this guide, in plain language.

    Tendon
    A thin cable that pulls. In the body it connects muscle to bone; here it connects the servo drum to the fingertip.
    Flexion / extension
    Bending the finger toward the palm / straightening it.
    MCP, PIP, DIP
    The three finger joints from base to tip. The thumb has MCP and IP.
    Volar / dorsal
    Palm side / back-of-hand side.
    Moment arm (d)
    The distance from a joint's axis to the line of the tendon. Torque at the joint = force × d.
    Tendon travel (excursion)
    How far the tendon must be pulled to bend the finger by a given amount.
    Underactuated
    Fewer motors than joints. One tendon moves three joints.
    Bowden sheath
    A tube the tendon slides inside, like a bicycle brake cable. It lets the cable bend around the wrist without changing its working length.
    Drum
    A round spool on the servo. The tendon winds on it, so travel = radius × angle.
    Stall torque
    The largest turning force a motor makes when it is blocked. Measured in kg·cm; 1 kg·cm ≈ 0.098 N·m.
    Servo pulse (PWM)
    The control signal: a pulse of about 0.5 to 2.4 ms, 50 times per second. Pulse width sets the angle.
    Back-drivable
    Whether pushing the output can turn the motor. Hobby servos mostly are not, so they hold position when unpowered.
    Common ground
    Joining the 0 V of separate supplies so signals between them have the same reference.
    Pull-up resistor
    Holds an input HIGH until a button pulls it LOW. The Arduino has them built in (INPUT_PULLUP).
    Debounce
    Ignoring the few milliseconds of rapid on-off flicker when a mechanical button closes.
    Voltage divider
    Two resistors in series that output a fixed fraction of a voltage. 10 kΩ + 10 kΩ halves it.
    Decoupling capacitor
    A capacitor across the supply that delivers short current spikes and keeps the voltage steady.
    Slow-blow fuse (T)
    A fuse that tolerates short start-up surges but blows on a lasting overload or short.
    Latching switch
    Stays in its new position after you let go. An E-stop must latch.
    State machine
    A program that is always in one named state and moves between states on clear conditions.
    Brownout
    A short drop in supply voltage that makes electronics misbehave or reset.
    Baud rate
    Serial communication speed. Both sides must match: 115200 here.
    CSV
    Comma-separated values: a plain text table that Excel opens directly.
    Calibration
    Measuring the real OPEN and CLOSED angles of your mechanism and writing them into the program.
